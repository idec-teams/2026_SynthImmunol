Result 1. Functional enrichment of the B cell display library and identification of top candidates

We sought to design a de novo protein that selectively binds the IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex and blocks gp130 engagement (Fig. 1a). The protein comprises three segments: A targets the gp130- binding interface on IL-6; B targets IL-6Rα Trp249, which participates in gp130 binding; and C targets Trp296 in the membrane-proximal region of IL-6Rα.

Using the crystal structure of the IL-6/sIL-6Rα/gp130 complex (PDB ID: 1P9M), we defined three target epitopes and corresponding hotspot constraints. RFdiffusion generated 400 backbones per site across 40 batches, yielding 1,200 backbones in total. ProteinMPNN generated 10 sequences per backbone, which were ranked by their mean per-residue scores; approximately 78 candidates were retained (Extended Data Table 1). AlphaFold-predicted complexes were filtered using a target-aligned binder RMSD < 10 Å and either a binder pTM > 0.7 or a minimum interchain PAE < 5 Å. The three highest-ranking designs per site yielded nine segment designs (Fig. 1b), which were combinatorially assembled into 27 full-length sequences (Extended Data Table 2).

Each design was fused to the FcRγ transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains to enable surface display and receptor-mediated internalization, and the lentiviral library was transduced into Raji cells and puromycin-selected to establish a stable library, with surface expression confirmed by flow cytometry (Fig. 1c)

Library-transduced cells were incubated with the biotinylated IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex and stained with streptavidin-APC (Extended Data Fig. 2). A membrane-impermeable fluorescence quencher was then applied to suppress surface-associated fluorescence, allowing retained intracellular APC fluorescence to serve as a readout of complex internalization. Cells were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting according to this signal.

Following the final sorting round, full-length inserts were amplified from genomic DNA using primers targeting conserved vector regions and subjected to long-read sequencing. Of 19,606 valid reads, 14,894 contained complete sequences, corresponding to 75.97% of valid reads (Extended Data Tables 3-4).

Of 19,606 valid reads, 14,894 contained complete sequences, corresponding to 75.97% of valid reads (Extended Data Tables 3-4). A2B3C3 was the most abundant variant, with 2,530 reads (17.0% of full-length sequences), followed by A2B3C2 with 1,157 reads (7.8%) and A3B2C2 with 1,012 reads (6.8%). Together, these variants accounted for 31.6% of full-length sequences after screening. Each exceeded the 3.7% frequency expected under equal representation of the 27 variants, identifying them as the top candidates from the functional screen (Fig. 1d). We also evaluated the top three variants using AlphaFold3 (AF3) structural modeling. AF3 predictions showed that A2B3C3 exhibited the largest buried surface area and formed hydrophobic contacts with both IL-6 and sIL-6Rα (Extended Data Tables 5–7).

Result 2. Structural interface and molecular dynamics analyses identify A2B3C3 as the starting sequence for directed evolution

To further assess the binding of the candidates to the IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex, we first performed static interface analyses of representative optimized structures for each system using Rosetta. A2B3C3 exhibited the most favorable interface energy (−16.285 Rosetta energy units [REU]) and the largest buried interface area (2,423.6 Å²), indicating that it formed an extensive and energetically favorable binding interface with the IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex (Fig. 2a-c). Although A3B2C2 had a relatively smaller interface area, it exhibited the most favorable interface energy density (−0.958) and the highest interface packing score (0.459), indicating a more compact interface with interactions concentrated within a smaller contact area (Fig. 2d-f). The static interface metrics of A2B3C2 were generally intermediate: its predicted interface energy was slightly more favorable than that of gp130, whereas its interface packing was comparable to that of gp130.

To determine whether these static binding conformations could be maintained in solution, we performed 100-ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the three candidate complexes and the gp130 reference complex. A2B3C3 maintained an extensive and stable binding interface throughout the simulation, exhibiting the lowest receptor-side root-mean-square deviation (RMSD; 1.34 ± 0.24 Å) and the largest mean buried surface area (31.82 ± 1.73 nm²) (Fig. 2g-j, Extended Data Fig. 3). Radar-plot analysis further showed relatively limited fluctuations in its interfacial van der Waals and electrostatic energies and in residue B factors, indicating that its overall conformation remained well constrained (Fig. 2m).

A3B2C2 had the lowest candidate-protein RMSD (2.23 ± 0.55 Å) and the highest number of interfacial hydrogen bonds (13.34 ± 2.60), consistent with stabilization through a tightly organized polar interaction network despite its smaller interface (Fig. 2g-l). A2B3C2 underwent greater candidate-side conformational rearrangement but retained its interface during the later simulation stages. Its buried surface area and hydrogen-bond retention over the final 20 ns were slightly higher than those of gp130.

Overall, the Rosetta static interface analyses and MD simulations yielded concordant results. The resulting computational prioritization was A2B3C3 (candidate 1) > A3B2C2 (candidate 3) > A2B3C2 (candidate 2) > the gp130 reference complex (Fig. 2; Extended Data Table 8). On the basis of its sequencing enrichment and overall performance in the MD-based evaluation, A2B3C3 was selected as the starting sequence (TOP1) for the second round of directed evolution and screening.

Result 3. dCas9-AID*Δ-driven directed evolution of A2B3C3 yields a potent IL-6/sIL- 6Rα-capturing molecule

Building on A2B3C3, which was identified through computational design and B cell surfacedisplay screening, we initiated directed evolution to further optimize this lead sequence. Localized point mutations were introduced into the A2, B3, and C3 coding regions, and binding to and uptake of the IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex were used as selection pressures to iteratively enrich full-length variants with enhanced complex-capture capacity, thereby improving their ability to compete with gp130.

To enable programmable hypermutation of A2B3C3 in Raji B cells, we developed dCas9- AID*Δ by combining design features of CRISPR-X and targeted AID-mediated mutagenesis (TAM). CRISPR-X uses sgRNAs containing two MS2 aptamers to recruit MS2-AID fusion proteins. Its hyperactive AID*Δ variant incorporates three gain-of-function substitutions (K10E, T82I, and E156G) and deletion of the three C-terminal residues8 . TAM instead directly fuses a P182X-truncated AID variant, termed AIDx, to dCas9 to target mutagenesis to sgRNA-defined regions9 . Following this direct fusion architecture, we fused the hyperactive AID*Δ variant to dCas9 to generate dCas9-AID*Δ, coupling programmable DNA targeting with enhanced AID activity (Fig. 3b).

For the second round of directed evolution, full-length A2B3C3 identified in the firstround screen was used as a single evolutionary unit. The A2, B3, and C3 binding segments were assembled in frame by Golden Gate cloning to generate a continuous open reading frame, which was subsequently linked to the transmembrane and intracellular signaling modules for surface display in Raji B cells (Fig. 3a)10. sgRNAs were designed to target the A2, B3, and C3 coding regions and direct dCas9-AID*Δ-mediated sequence diversification to the three corresponding regions. Thus, rather than generating predetermined substitutions at individual nucleotide positions, the three sgRNAs established three locally enriched mutational windows within the same full-length A2B3C3 coding sequence. Following activation of the targeted mutagenesis system in Raji B cells, the cells were incubated with a fluorescently labeled IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex and subjected to flow cytometric sorting based on retained complex-associated fluorescence.

To characterize AID*Δ-generated mutational profiles, PacBio HiFi full-length reads were aligned to the Golden Gate-assembled A2B3C3 reference sequence, and nucleotide substitution frequencies were calculated at each position11. Using an operational threshold of 0.1%, substitutions were preferentially enriched within the three sgRNA-targeted regions in A2, B3, and C3 and their flanking sequences, whereas most distal positions remained at or below this threshold (Fig. 3c). Substitutions included G>A, C>T, G>C, C>A, and C>G across the targeted regions.

To further characterize the nucleotide preference of the induced mutations, substitution events detected within the A2, B3, and C3 regions were classified according to the corresponding reference nucleotide. Substitutions originating from C or G accounted for 54.7%, 68.6%, and 65.0% of all substitutions detected in the A2, B3, and C3 regions, respectively (Fig. 3d), indicating that the mutations predominantly originated from C:G base pairs. Because AID directly catalyzes cytosine deamination in single-stranded DNA, the resulting mutation may be recorded as either a C- or G-derived substitution depending on the orientation of the reference strand. This distribution was therefore consistent with the biochemical mechanism of AIDmediated cytosine deamination.

The substitution outcomes of mutations originating from C, G, A, or T were subsequently analyzed separately. In addition to the canonical C>T and complementary G>A transitions, the dCas9-AID*Δ system generated a broader spectrum of nucleotide substitutions through subsequent DNA replication and mismatch-repair processes. The resulting candidate sequences were prioritized for variant reconstruction and further evaluation of IL-6/sIL-6Rα binding and cellular internalization (Fig. 3c).

Result 4. Second-round evolution enhances interfacial interactions between candidate proteins and the IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex

To further investigate the structural basis underlying the stable association of the evolved candidates with the IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex, we used AlphaFold 3 to generate structural models of the corresponding protein complexes (Fig. 4a-c). Computational alanine scanning was subsequently performed to identify hotspot residues at the predicted binding interfaces (Fig. 4d; Extended Data Table 12). All three evolved candidates exhibited substantially increased buried surface areas relative to the first-round candidates. Among them, S2 formed the largest buried interface, with a buried surface area of 2,146.193 Å², compared with 1,744.917 Å² for S1 and 1,423.955 Å² for S3. Although the three candidates showed no appreciable difference in their propensity to form hydrogen bonds, S2 formed a greater number of hydrophobic contacts (Extended Data Table 9-11), which may contribute to the stabilization of its bound conformation.

Separate analysis of the candidate interfaces with IL-6 and sIL-6Rα revealed that all three candidates adopted a relatively conserved interaction pattern at the IL-6-binding interface, involving the Tyr13-Arg6-Leu1 interaction network. By contrast, the C-terminal helical region of S2 inserted more deeply into the sIL-6Rα interface, resulting in a marked increase in contacts with hotspot residues. Among these residues, sIL-6Rα Trp169 exhibited the highest contact count (1,468) and was identified as the predominant hotspot residue at the interface.

We designated the selected candidate S2 as STAR6. To characterize the binding properties of STAR6, we measured its affinity for the IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex using SPR. As the concentration of the IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex increased from 15.625 to 2,000 pM, the SPR response of STAR increased in a concentration-dependent manner. Kinetic analysis yielded an equilibrium dissociation constant (K_D) of approximately 52 pM, demonstrating that STAR bound the IL- 6/sIL-6Rα complex with high affinity (Fig. 5a). Thus, directed evolution improved the affinity of STAR6 for the IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex by approximately 14.3-fold relative to A2B3C3 (Fig. 2n). Having established that STAR6 recognized the IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex with high affinity and competitively inhibited its interaction with gp130, we next displayed STAR6 on the surface of macrophages as an extracellular recognition module coupled to the cytoplasmic domain of FcRγ (Fig. 5b). We termed these engineered macrophages STAR-Trap to reflect their intended ability to capture and clear the complex through receptor-mediated internalization. Stable establishment of the STAR-Trap population and surface expression of the STAR6–FcRγ receptor were confirmed by flow cytometry (Fig. 5c).

To assess STAR-Trap-mediated internalization of the IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex, STAR-Trap were incubated with biotinylated IL-6/sIL-6Rα complexes fluorescently labeled with streptavidin-APC. Extracellular fluorescence was then quenched, leaving only intracellular signals detectable. Flow cytometry revealed a higher proportion of fluorescence-positive cells and greater mean intracellular fluorescence intensity in STAR-Trap cells than in control macrophages, indicating enhanced internalization of the complex (Fig. 5d).

Confocal fluorescence imaging provided further spatial evidence for the intracellular fate of the captured complex. STAR-Trap exhibited prominent intracellular signals derived from the fluorescent IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex probe, whereas little probe fluorescence was detected in control macrophages (Fig. 5e). Moreover, the internalized complex showed substantial colocalization with LysoTracker-positive compartments, indicating that STAR-Trap-mediated uptake was followed by trafficking of the complex to lysosomes. Together, these findings demonstrate that STAR6 combines high-affinity recognition of the IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex with competitive inhibition of its interaction with gp130. Incorporation of STAR6 into an FcRγ- containing macrophage surface receptor equipped STAR-Trap cells with the capacity to capture, internalize and deliver the complex to lysosomal compartments.

Result 6. STAR-Trap engineering biases macrophages toward an M1-like phenotype

We next examined whether STAR-Trap engineering altered the polarization state of macrophages. Flow cytometry was used to evaluate the M1-associated markers HLA-DR and CD86, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), and the M2-associated markers CD206, Arg-1, and IL-10 (Fig. 6a-b). Compared with control macrophages, STAR-Trap cells exhibited increased HLA-DR and CD86 expression together with elevated intracellular ROS levels. In contrast, CD206 and IL-10 levels were reduced, whereas Arg-1 showed only a minimal change. Collectively, the coordinated increase in M1-associated features and reduction in several M2- associated markers indicate that STAR-Trap engineering was associated with a shift toward an M1-like macrophage phenotype.

Result 7. STAR-Trap selectively suppresses IL-6 trans-signaling while preserving classical signaling

To determine whether STAR-Trap selectively inhibited IL-6 trans-signaling while preserving classical signaling, we established two separate cell-based assays to evaluate classical signaling and trans-signaling, respectively. To assess IL-6 trans-signaling, the IL- 6/sIL-6Rα complex was preincubated with wild-type macrophages or STAR-Trap. After removal of the macrophages, the resulting supernatants were applied to human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). The IL-6/sIL-6Rα complex induced robust STAT3 phosphorylation and increased MCP-1 and ICAM-1 expression in HUVECs (Fig. 7a). These responses remained pronounced in HUVECs treated with supernatants collected after preincubation of the complex with wild-type macrophages, but were markedly reduced in HUVECs treated with supernatants from the STAR-Trap condition, as demonstrated by decreased intracellular pSTAT3 levels and reduced MCP-1 and ICAM-1 expression.

To assess classical signaling, free IL-6 was preincubated with either wild-type macrophages or STAR-Trap. Cell-free supernatants were subsequently collected and applied to U266 human multiple myeloma cells, which express both membrane-bound IL-6Rα (mIL-6Rα) and gp130. Free IL-6 induced robust STAT3 and ERK1/2 phosphorylation together with increased SOCS3 expression in U266 cells (Fig. 7b). These responses remained largely unchanged when U266 cells were treated with supernatants collected after preincubation of free IL-6 with either wild-type or STAR-Trap. By contrast, treatment with the IL-6R-blocking antibody tocilizumab substantially reduced pSTAT3, pERK1/2, and SOCS3 levels in U266 cells. Thus, STAR-Trap did not measurably impair classical signaling mediated by free IL-6.

Together, these findings demonstrate that STAR-Trap selectively removes the IL-6/sIL- 6Rα complex and suppresses the associated trans-signaling response while largely preserving classical signaling mediated by free IL-6.
